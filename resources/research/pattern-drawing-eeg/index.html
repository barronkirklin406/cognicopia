Pattern Drawing and the Brain: What EEG Studies of Zentangle Show

Two brain-activity studies, four controlled trials, and what they do and do not tell a memory care team

8 min read15 sourcesReviewed September 30, 2026

Written for: Medical directors, Activity directors, Administrators and boards

Key takeaways

  • Two EEG studies recorded brain activity while adults without dementia drew Zentangle patterns. Theta, alpha, beta and gamma activity rose during the session, and the 2026 study read the overall pattern as focused engagement rather than simple relaxation.1,2
  • In older adults, small controlled trials found less depression after six Zentangle sessions, and less anxiety with more happiness after an eight-week course in a nursing home.3,4
  • Coloring structured patterns lowers anxiety about as well as free drawing. The benefit seems to come from absorbing, structured art-making, not from one particular design.5,6
  • No EEG study has included people living with dementia, and the Cochrane review of art therapy in dementia found too little evidence to judge.7
  • Offer pattern drawing as a calm, structured, adult activity that residents enjoy, not as a treatment with a promised effect on the brain.

Why this question matters1 min

In brief: Pattern drawing is common in activity programs, and the claims made for it range from reasonable to overstated.

Pattern drawing and pattern coloring are fixtures of activity calendars. Families, surveyors and boards increasingly ask whether they “work”, and the claims made online range from reasonable (it is calming) to overstated (it “rewires the brain”). This summary separates what has been measured from what has been assumed.

The Zentangle Method is a structured way of drawing: small repeated patterns, drawn in pen one stroke at a time inside a small tile, taught as a relaxed and focused practice. Cognicopia’s pattern and botanical pages borrow the principle (repeated, predictable shapes inside clear boundaries) for coloring. They are not the Zentangle Method itself, and they have not been studied directly.

What alpha activity is, and is not1 min

In brief: Higher alpha activity accompanies calm, focused attention. On its own it is not a measure of benefit.

An EEG records the brain’s electrical rhythms from the scalp. The alpha rhythm, roughly 8 to 12 cycles a second, is prominent when a person is awake and relaxed, and it also shifts with attention and visual processing. Other bands (delta, theta, beta, gamma) have their own associations, and all of them vary with the task, the person and the recording.

The most useful comparison is meditation. A systematic review of 56 EEG studies of mindfulness, with 1,715 participants, found mindfulness most often associated with higher alpha and theta power, although not every study found it.8

Reading brain-rhythm findings

A rise in alpha during an activity is consistent with calm, focused attention. It does not show that the activity improves memory, and a change recorded during one session does not show a lasting change.

The Zentangle EEG studies2 min

In brief: Both studies recorded single sessions in adults without dementia. Both found the brain engaged and focused while drawing.

Study at a glance

Usman et al., 20241

Design
One Zentangle session. Single group, with no comparison activity.
Participants
30 healthy men, average age 24, none of whom had tried Zentangle before.
Measured
EEG in three 3-minute segments (early, middle and late in the session), and self-ratings of concentration, calm and stress on a 10-point scale.
Found
Delta activity fell, and theta, alpha, beta and gamma activity rose, with changes in how brain regions worked together. Participants rated themselves more focused, calmer and less stressed.
Caveats
Young healthy men only, no comparison activity, one session, and self-ratings.

Study at a glance

Lin et al., 20262

Design
One 15-minute Zentangle session, analyzed in six consecutive phases, with EEG and heart recordings (ECG).
Participants
A laboratory sample; the study did not include people living with dementia.
Measured
Brain rhythms, frontal alpha asymmetry, connections between regions, and heart-rate variability, phase by phase.
Found
From the second phase on, frontal alpha asymmetry leaned to the left, a pattern linked in the literature to approach motivation and positive feeling. Heart-rate variability first showed less parasympathetic influence, then shifts consistent with focused, effortful engagement. The authors read the late-session rise in delta activity as internal processing with sustained engagement, “rather than unequivocal relaxation”.
Caveats
A single session, in people without dementia.

An earlier study measured alpha activity before and after 20 minutes of drawing with oil pastels in 10 adults (6 artists and 4 non-artists) and found alpha differences in several brain regions after drawing.9

Read together, these studies describe a brain that is engaged and focused while drawing patterns, in a way that resembles mindfulness practice. That is a plausible basis for the calm many facilitators observe. It is not evidence that pattern drawing improves cognition, and none of it was measured in people living with dementia.

Stress, reward and anxiety studies of art-making1 min

In brief: Art-making lowered cortisol and engaged the brain’s reward areas. Structured coloring and free drawing eased anxiety about equally.

  • Cortisol: 39 healthy adults aged 18 to 59 spent 45 minutes making art with an art therapist. Cortisol, a stress hormone, fell significantly afterward, in about three quarters of participants, whatever their experience with art.10
  • Reward: brain imaging (functional near-infrared spectroscopy) during 3 minutes each of coloring, doodling and free drawing showed all three activating the brain’s reward pathway compared with rest, doodling the most.11
  • Anxiety: after an anxiety-raising task, 84 students colored a mandala, a plaid pattern or a blank page. Anxiety fell about equally for the mandala and plaid groups, and more than for the blank page.5
  • A meta-analysis of 8 studies (578 participants) found mandala coloring and free drawing equally effective for anxiety in the moment, and found signs that small studies overstate the effect.6

A 2024 review of art therapy and neuroscience describes the evidence, its limits and the myths that have grown around it, and notes that how art therapy works is still poorly understood.12

Trials in older adults1 min

In brief: Three small controlled trials of Zentangle, one in a nursing home, found less depression or anxiety.

Study at a glance

Chan & Lo, 20243

Design
Randomized trial against a waitlist.
Participants
46 older adults living in the community with mild to moderate depression.
Measured
Depression (PHQ-9) and self-compassion, before, after six sessions, and six weeks later.
Found
Depression fell and self-compassion rose in the Zentangle group compared with the waitlist, and the gains held at six weeks.
Caveats
Community-dwelling adults with depression, not dementia. A waitlist, not another activity, was the comparison.

Study at a glance

McCord et al., 20254

Design
Randomized block design with a control group, in one regional Australian nursing home during COVID-19 visiting restrictions.
Participants
20 nursing home residents.
Measured
Anxiety, happiness and manual dexterity before and after an eight-week Zentangle course that included mindfulness techniques.
Found
Anxiety fell 7% and happiness rose 26% in the Zentangle group, both significant. 71% said they would continue or recommend it. Dexterity results suggested a possible protective effect. Residents with mild to moderate cognitive changes enjoyed the course most.
Caveats
A small trial in one home, at an unusual time.

A pilot randomized trial in adults from the general population found less negative feeling after Zentangle classes than on a waitlist, both straight after and two weeks later.13 A workplace program for 40 rural healthcare workers reported less work stress and frustration.14

What the evidence says about dementia1 min

In brief: Art therapy in dementia is under-studied. One recent trial is encouraging; none tested pattern drawing.

The 2018 Cochrane review of art therapy for people with dementia found two studies with 60 participants analyzed, out of 88 randomized, and concluded that there is insufficient evidence about its efficacy.7

Since then, a randomized trial of a creative arts and storytelling program for 78 people with mild to moderate dementia and their caregivers found fewer behavioral and psychological symptoms, better communication and quality of life, and less caregiver distress and burden than a social-contact comparison, lasting at least 24 weeks.15 It was not a pattern-drawing program.

What this means for your community

  • Offer pattern drawing and coloring as an adult, structured activity that residents enjoy, and match the complexity to the person (Cognicopia’s three support tiers).
  • The measured sessions were short, about 15 minutes. Short, unhurried sessions are a reasonable starting point.
  • Record what you see (attention, calm, conversation, time engaged). Over a few weeks that record is your own evidence.
  • Use pattern drawing alongside approaches with stronger evidence, such as cognitive stimulation, reminiscence and music.
  • Keep family and marketing language to what is known. “Calming and enjoyable” is supported; “rewires the brain” is not.

What this evidence does not show

  • Both EEG studies recorded single sessions in people without dementia. The 2024 study included only healthy men, average age 24, and had no comparison activity.
  • Changes in brain rhythms during one session do not show a lasting benefit or any effect on memory.
  • The trials in older adults were small (20 to 46 people), and none studied moderate or advanced dementia.
  • Cognicopia’s own printed pattern pages have not been studied.
